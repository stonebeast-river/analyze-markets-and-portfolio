# Sector rotation and opportunity framework

Use this framework to scan broadly while concentrating detailed research on material changes.

## 1. Define the sector universe

- For mainland China, inspect at least financials, property and construction, consumer staples, consumer discretionary and services, healthcare, industrials and capital goods, technology/electronics/semiconductors, energy, materials, and utilities, plus material policy or thematic groups.
- For Hong Kong, inspect at least internet platforms and technology, financials, property, consumer, healthcare, telecom and utilities, and energy, while accounting for mainland-linked businesses and index concentration.
- For the United States, inspect all eleven GICS sectors plus market-moving themes such as regional banks, semiconductors, AI infrastructure, homebuilders, or small caps when they cut across sectors.
- Add Europe, Japan, emerging markets, or commodities-linked sectors when they materially affect the global story.

Keep classifications consistent within a report. Do not compare incompatible sector schemes without explaining the mapping.

Completing the scan does not require printing every group. It requires checking the universe and then naming the groups that strengthened, weakened, or showed no material divergence. A macro label or broad index alone is not a sector result.

## 2. Run the daily breadth scan

Inspect, when reliable data are available:

1. relative performance versus the local broad index over one day, one week, and one to three months;
2. participation within the sector rather than only the largest constituent;
3. earnings estimates, margins, orders, prices, or other fundamental revisions;
4. valuation versus its own history, growth outlook, and credible peers;
5. policy, rates, currency, commodity, regulation, supply-chain, and company catalysts;
6. volume, flows, positioning, and crowding as supporting rather than decisive evidence.

Do not fabricate unavailable breadth, flow, or valuation data. Use `unknown` and lower confidence.

For each of mainland China, Hong Kong, and the United States, the output must still say:

- which named industries or themes led and lagged, or that differences were not material;
- whether one-day, one-week, and one-to-three-month evidence agree or conflict;
- whether participation is broad or concentrated;
- which fundamental, valuation, policy, flow, or volume evidence confirms or challenges the price signal;
- a rotation state and confidence level.

When historical or public data are incomplete, report the exact missing window or evidence type. Do not replace a missing sector scan with a regional-market paragraph.

## 3. Classify rotation state

Use one of these states only when evidence supports it:

- `Emerging`: catalyst and fundamentals are improving, but leadership and participation remain early.
- `Broadening`: performance and participation expand beyond a few leaders while fundamentals confirm.
- `Mature`: thesis is widely recognized; valuation or positioning leaves less margin for error.
- `Crowded`: price, valuation, flows, or consensus imply asymmetric downside if evidence disappoints.
- `Fading`: relative strength, breadth, or fundamentals deteriorate and prior catalysts lose force.
- `Noise`: move is too short, narrow, illiquid, or unsupported to classify as rotation.

Do not label a one-day bounce as `Emerging` without another independent evidence type.

## 4. Trigger a deep dive

Deep-dive a sector when at least one event is material and another evidence type can confirm or challenge it:

- major policy or regulatory change;
- earnings, guidance, order, margin, inventory, or price revision;
- important commodity, rate, or currency shock;
- sustained relative performance with improving breadth;
- unusual reliable flow or positioning change;
- valuation regime change;
- supply-chain disruption or technological adoption shift.

If only one weak signal exists, put the sector on the watchlist instead.

## 5. Form an opportunity thesis

For each research opportunity, answer:

- What is mispriced or underappreciated?
- Why might the market recognize it now rather than later?
- Which observable evidence supports the thesis?
- What is the expected horizon?
- Which asset, sector index, or product type best expresses the thesis, without recommending an unverified product?
- What competing opportunity offers a better risk-reward profile?
- What confirms, weakens, or invalidates the thesis?
- What could make waiting superior to acting now?

Separate four opportunity types:

- fundamental improvement;
- policy or liquidity transmission;
- valuation mean reversion with a catalyst;
- defensive or diversification value under a defined risk scenario.

Avoid presenting pure momentum, low valuation, high yield, or a popular narrative as sufficient by itself.

Every daily candidate must explicitly include one of the four opportunity types and an evidence confidence of `high`, `medium`, or `low`. Compare it with at least one credible alternative or explain why cash/waiting has the better current risk-reward. Use exactly `worth dedicated research`, `watch`, or `not yet` for the follow-up field.

## 6. Control daily-report depth

In the recurring daily report:

- keep the opportunity thesis and evidence complete but compact;
- name only the likely investable route, such as a broad asset, sector index, thematic index, commodity exposure, or product category;
- mark the candidate as `worth dedicated research`, `watch`, or `not yet`;
- do not perform a complete index-methodology review or compare specific funds and ETFs;
- do not imply that a follow-up flag authorizes a purchase.

The daily report should reveal possibilities and the conditions that make them interesting. The user decides which possibility deserves deeper work.

On routine days, prefer two to four well-supported candidates over a long speculative list. If nothing clears the evidence threshold, say `no qualified new opportunity` and preserve existing watch items. On event days, add candidates only when the event creates a distinct thesis rather than another expression of the same trade.

## 7. Expand products only on demand

When the user explicitly chooses an opportunity for deeper research:

1. Revalidate that the opportunity thesis and trigger conditions still hold.
2. Find credible indices or other investable expressions. Compare methodology, constituents, concentration, rebalancing, geographic and currency exposure, historical behavior, and fit with the thesis.
3. Compare verified on-exchange ETFs and off-exchange funds or ETF feeders when relevant, including:
   - exact share class;
   - management, custody, service, subscription, and redemption fees;
   - fund size, age, liquidity, and trading spread for exchange-traded products;
   - tracking difference, tracking error, replication method, cash drag, and premium or discount;
   - subscription limits, QDII quota or valuation lag, currency exposure, platform availability, and trading convenience;
   - overlap with the user's existing portfolio and likely effect on portfolio risk.
4. Produce a short candidate list with strengths, weaknesses, suitable use cases, and evidence confidence.
5. Give observable confirmation, waiting, review, and invalidation conditions rather than an automatic order instruction.

Do not continue from opportunity discovery to product due diligence unless the user requests it. Never execute or alter an investment plan merely because a candidate ranks well.

## 8. Preserve independence from the portfolio

Complete the sector scan and opportunity matrix before opening the personalized appendix. Include attractive or risky sectors regardless of whether the user owns them. In the appendix, map the independent sector conclusions to existing holdings without deleting or rewriting the broader opportunity set.
